**Best SEL and STEM Books for Kids Ages 6-9**

Kids don't experience "STEM" and "feelings" as separate categories. A
seven-year-old building a wobbly block tower and a seven-year-old working
through disappointment when it falls over are having the same moment.
The best books for this age don't pick one lane — they let a kid be
curious *and* frustrated *and* proud, sometimes in the same chapter.

That's the thinking behind why we believe STEM stories build braver
kids — if you want the fuller philosophy on that, it's worth a read. This
list is the practical version: eight real books, ages 6-9, where the
science or engineering and the emotional honesty are both doing real
work.

**1. Rosie Revere, Engineer** — *Andrea Beaty, illustrated by David
Roberts*
Rosie builds inventions in secret because she's embarrassed by one that
failed in front of her class. STEM side: real engineering
process — problem, prototype, failure, revision. SEL side: the story
follows Rosie learning that a failed first attempt isn't the end of the
idea, just the first draft of it.

**2. Ada Twist, Scientist** — *Andrea Beaty, illustrated by David Roberts*
Ada asks so many questions her parents run out of answers. STEM side: a
genuine, kid-scale introduction to the scientific method — observe,
question, test. SEL side: the story shows a family learning to make room
for a kid whose curiosity doesn't have an off switch, instead of shutting
it down.

**3. Iggy Peck, Architect** — *Andrea Beaty, illustrated by David Roberts*
Iggy has been building structures since he was two — and gets told to
stop. STEM side: real architectural concepts, explained through what a
kid can actually picture (bridges, towers, structural balance). SEL side:
Iggy's confidence in his own gift, even when an adult in the story
doesn't value it.

**4. The Most Magnificent Thing** — *Ashley Spires*
A girl has a brilliant idea for something she wants to build. It doesn't
work. It still doesn't work. STEM side: this is the design process in
its purest form — build, test, fail, adjust, repeat. SEL side: the story
gives real weight to the frustration of failing repeatedly, and lets the
character take a real break before trying again, instead of pretending
persistence is easy.

**5. Counting on Katherine: How Katherine Johnson Saved Apollo 13** —
*Helaine Becker, illustrated by Dow Phumiruk*
The true story of the NASA mathematician whose calculations helped bring
the Apollo 13 crew home safely. STEM side: real mathematics, real
history, a real mission. SEL side: Katherine Johnson's confidence in her
own ability, in a room and a moment that doubted her.

**6. Astro Girl** — *Ken Wilson-Max*
A girl trains to become an astronaut, with her dad supporting her through
every part of the process — including the scary parts. STEM side: a real,
accurate look at what astronaut training actually involves. SEL side: the
story treats her fear as normal, not something to hide, and lets her
parent's steady support be part of the story instead of background noise.

**7. What Do You Do With a Problem?** — *Kobi Yamada, illustrated by Mae
Besom*
This one isn't a STEM book in the literal sense — there's no science
experiment or invention in it. It earns its place on this list because
the mindset it explores — facing a problem instead of avoiding it,
staying with it until it turns into something else — is the same mindset
every STEM book on this list is really asking a kid to practice. SEL
side: reframing something scary as something workable.

**8. Adventures of Charlotte and Henry: The Mariana Trench** — *Andrew
Signore*
Charlotte and Henry travel to the deepest place on Earth. STEM side: real
ocean science and deep-sea exploration, woven into an actual adventure
rather than delivered as a lecture. SEL side: at a real classroom
read-aloud, a second grader spontaneously used a breathing technique from
this book mid-story, without anyone prompting them. That's not something
we planned for — it's just what happened when the story gave a kid a
concrete way to sit with something big and scary instead of shutting
down.

**One More Thing**

None of these eight books "fix" anything, and that's not the promise
here. What they have in common is that they let a kid be smart and
overwhelmed at the same time, without picking one to be more important
than the other. That's the actual overlap between STEM and SEL at this
age — not two separate subjects, just one honest story.

*Big Places. Brave Hearts.*

**10 Amazon Rainforest Facts for Kids (Ages 6-9)**

The Amazon isn't just big. It's the kind of big that's hard to picture
until you start putting numbers next to it — and once you do, it gets
strange fast: pink dolphins, frogs that could stop a heart, a river with
more water in it than almost anything else on Earth.

Here are 10 real facts about the Amazon rainforest that hold up to a
second look — the kind of facts you can hand a kid without having to
double-check them later.

**1. It's the size of a small continent.** The Amazon rainforest covers
roughly 2.1 million square miles — about 5.5 million square
kilometers — making it commonly described as the largest rainforest on
Earth.

**2. It doesn't belong to just one country.** The Amazon stretches
across parts of nine countries: Brazil, Peru, Colombia, Venezuela,
Ecuador, Bolivia, Guyana, Suriname, and French Guiana.

**3. Its river carries more water than almost anything else on the
planet.** By volume, the Amazon River moves more water than the next
several biggest rivers on Earth combined.

**4. Its dolphins are pink.** Botos, or pink river dolphins, live in the
Amazon's rivers — one of the only freshwater dolphin species anywhere in
the world.

**5. Some of its frogs are dangerously toxic.** Poison dart frogs live on
the rainforest floor, and some species are among the most toxic animals
on the planet.

**6. Nobody's even sure exactly how many kinds of trees are there.**
Scientists have identified thousands of different tree species in the
Amazon — one major scientific survey estimated as many as 16,000
distinct kinds.

**7. Its otters are sometimes called "river wolves."** Giant otters can
grow up to six feet long and travel in family groups along Amazon
rivers.

**8. Its piranhas aren't what the movies say.** Despite their fearsome
reputation, most piranha species are scavengers, not hunters — real
attacks on people are extremely rare.

**9. Its birds are some of the loudest, brightest animals on Earth.**
Toucans and scarlet macaws are Amazon icons, known for colors that stand
out from a mile away and calls that carry through the trees.

**10. People have called it home for thousands of years.** Indigenous
peoples have lived in the Amazon rainforest for thousands of years,
building deep knowledge of its plants and animals long before outside
scientists ever arrived to study it.

**One More Thing**

We pair real places like this with an adventure story on purpose. Facts
alone can feel like homework. A story alone can feel made up. Put them
together, and a kid gets both — something true, and something that pulls
them forward to the next page.

*Big Places. Brave Hearts.*
